New surface targets for osteosarcoma

Built November 13–14, 2026 from open data: 88 TARGET-OS tumour RNA-seq samples (NCI GDC), GTEx v8 normal-tissue medians, Human Protein Atlas surface annotation, ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 (trial statuses re-checked November 14, 2026). Script: tools/surface_targets.py. Full table: data/osteosarcoma_surface_target_candidates.csv (2,434 genes). Working notes: research_notes/Surface targets/notes.md. Updated November 19, 2026 with a protein-level check of the leads (research_notes/Surface targets/protein_check.md) and new trial records; see the change log.

Summary

Antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and CAR-T cells need a protein that sits on the tumour cell surface and is scarce in vital organs. We scored every surface protein expressed in 88 osteosarcoma tumours by how high and how consistently it is expressed and how much of it appears in normal heart, brain, lung, gut and other vital tissues. Of the antigens already in osteosarcoma programmes, B7-H3 scores well, but HER2, GD2 synthase, IL11RA and EGFR score below average, because in these tumours they are expressed at low levels while being abundant in some normal tissues. The top hit, LRRC15, already has four registered trials, two of them recruiting, including one specifically in relapsed osteosarcoma. Below it the screen points to IFITM5, ADAM12, MRC2, PTK7 and CD248. This is RNA, not protein, from 88 diagnostic biopsies, compared against adult tissue that contains no bone, so every lead needs checking in tumour tissue before anyone acts on it. A protein check in November 2026 found that, among the five leads, only LRRC15 has been measured as protein in patient osteosarcomas, in three studies of patient tumours (Cui 2020, O'Reilly 2026, Moquin-Beaudry 2026) plus one of patient-derived xenografts (Mooney 2026); PTK7 has been stained only in two xenografts and two cell lines, IFITM5 and ADAM12 have no human osteosarcoma protein data at all, and TMEM119, which our surface filter excluded, has surface staining in patient tumours.

How to read the labels. Key numbers carry one of these tags: - (registry): read directly from an official registry or public database: ClinicalTrials.gov, PubMed, GTEx, Human Protein Atlas, EMA/FDA approval records. - (paper, full text): we read the full paper. - (paper, abstract only): we read only the abstract. - (press release only): the only source is a press release or a company or institution web page. - (our calculation): we computed it from this repository's data files with the script named above. - (preprint, abstract only): we read only the abstract of a preprint, which has not been peer-reviewed.

Why this screen

No new agent has become standard treatment for osteosarcoma in the US since the 1980s; mifamurtide was approved in the EU in 2009 for non-metastatic disease (registry). Most of the drug trials are not antibodies at all. The most-trialled targets are blood-vessel receptors hit by multi-kinase pills (VEGFR2 in 57 trials) and PD-1 (at least 29) (our calculation; see the literature map). Antigen-directed agents are a smaller, newer group, and most of them aim at a handful of antigens, chiefly GD2 (22 osteosarcoma trials), B7-H3 (13), HER2 (7) and EGFR (5) (our calculation from the repository's drug map). Others have been tried or proposed: GPNMB's ADC completed a phase 2 trial in osteosarcoma that did not meet its goal (paper, abstract only), and we found no registered osteosarcoma trial against IL11RA, EPHA2 or ALPL (registry). Several of these antigens were chosen because an antibody already existed for another cancer. A systematic open-data pass is cheap, so the question is whether it turns up anything the field has not already landed on.

Method

The screen is a therapeutic-window calculation over three public datasets.

  1. Tumour expression. All 88 open-access RNA-seq samples in the NCI GDC project TARGET-OS (STAR gene counts, tpm_unstranded, protein-coding genes only; every one a primary tumour). For each gene: median TPM across tumours, quartiles, and the share of tumours above 5 TPM. A gene had to reach median 10 TPM to be considered.
  2. Surface filter. Human Protein Atlas annotation. A gene is kept if it is a predicted membrane protein. It is graded high confidence if the plasma membrane is one of its main HPA locations or it is a curated CD marker, medium if the plasma membrane appears only as a secondary location, and low otherwise. The headline list uses high plus medium.
  3. Normal tissue. GTEx v8 median TPM in each of 54 tissues. The window denominator is the highest median among 32 "vital" tissue entries: heart, lung, liver, kidney, 12 brain regions and the cervical spinal cord, tibial nerve, colon and small intestine, arteries, whole blood, oesophageal mucosa, stomach, pancreas, adrenal and skeletal muscle. The worst tissue across all 54 is reported separately, as is GTEx's cultured-fibroblast sample. For a mesenchymal tumour the fibroblast value is a useful comparator: an antigen that is also high in fibroblasts is probably a lineage marker rather than a tumour marker.
  4. Score. window_score = log2((tumour median + 1) / (worst vital normal + 1)) × (fraction of tumours expressing). The log ratio is the window; the multiplier penalises a gene that is high in a third of tumours and absent in the rest, which is of little use for a single-antigen agent.
  5. Context. For the leading candidates, ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 was searched for antibody, ADC, bispecific or CAR programmes naming the target in any cancer. Osteosarcoma literature depth was taken from this repository's evidence tables, which inherit their limitations (see the literature map).
  6. Comparison set. We chose these comparison genes ourselves; they were not pre-specified positives. They are the eight antigens with osteosarcoma programmes or proposals (B7-H3, GD2 via its synthase B4GALNT1, HER2, GPNMB, EGFR, IL11RA, EPHA2, ALPL) plus LRRC15, MMP14 and CDH11, which we added because they are known mesenchymal or sarcoma antigens. They are scored alongside the candidates and flagged in the output table.

Three things follow from this table.

The genes we expected to score well did. LRRC15, MMP14, B7-H3 and CDH11 come out at ranks 1, 7, 15 and 21 of 2,434, and B7-H3 is in the top 1%. This is a weak check, not a validation. We chose LRRC15, MMP14 and CDH11 knowing they are mesenchymal antigens, so their good ranks partly restate that choice, and the antigens actually in trials mostly rank poorly. None of the four is truly osteosarcoma-specific either. B7-H3 is found across many cancers and is 63 TPM in GTEx fibroblasts, and MMP14 is 237.5 TPM in tibial artery (registry).

Several current favourites score badly. HER2, GD2 synthase, IL11RA and EGFR rank in the bottom half. For HER2 and EGFR this matches the clinical record: both have underwhelmed in osteosarcoma, and HER2 expression in osteosarcoma has been disputed for twenty years. HER2 sits at 15 TPM in tumours against 130 TPM in tibial nerve and 21–38 TPM in heart (registry). That is the wrong direction for a target whose agents' dose-limiting toxicities include cardiac and nerve damage. Ignore the GD2 rank. GD2 is a glycolipid, not a protein, so the synthase mRNA is a poor proxy for how much antigen is on the cell. IL11RA at 6 TPM in 59% of tumours is a genuine concern.

ALPL shows the screen's biggest blind spot. It ranks 70th, held back by 598 TPM in whole blood (neutrophils are full of alkaline phosphatase) and 52 TPM in liver (registry). But GTEx contains no bone, cartilage or osteoblast tissue. Any bone-restricted antigen therefore looks cleaner here than it is. That applies most of all to IFITM5 below.

All 25 have "evidence at protein level" in HPA, meaning the protein itself has been observed, not merely predicted from the transcript (registry). "IHC" and "IF" are HPA's own reliability grades for its antibody staining and immunofluorescence. Enhanced and Supported are the strong grades; Uncertain means HPA itself does not trust the staining. Two genes with higher overall scores, PANX3 and TMEM119 (overall ranks 3 and 5), are not in this list because HPA gives them only low surface confidence.

TMEM119 should be considered despite the filter. A 2026 mass-spectrometry study of 22 osteosarcoma patient-derived xenografts ranked TMEM119 4th of 190 surface candidates, and on a tissue microarray of patient osteosarcomas found "robust staining" with "distinct surface staining", with limited staining in 21 of 22 normal paediatric tissues (tonsil the exception) (paper, full text; Mooney et al., Cell Rep 2026, PMID 42647168). In our screen it has 565 TPM in 98.9% of tumours (our calculation). So the HPA-based surface call was wrong for TMEM119, and the shortlist should be re-run with it admitted.

Changing the pseudocount or dropping the multiplier barely moves anything. Using all 54 tissues as the denominator moves PTK7 from 64 to 551 and CD248 from 98 to 1,230, because PTK7 is high in uterus (116 TPM) and CD248 in fat (365 TPM). The five leads, in plain terms

1. LRRC15: the top hit, and already in osteosarcoma trials

220 TPM in tumours, expressed in 97.7% of them, against 0.8 TPM in the worst vital tissue and 3.1 TPM in fibroblasts (our calculation). That is a 122-fold window as scored (272-fold on the raw medians, before the pseudocount), the best in the dataset. LRRC15 is a known ADC target, and AbbVie's samrotamab vedotin (ABBV-085) was an earlier attempt. ClinicalTrials.gov now lists four LRRC15 programmes (registry, November 14, 2026):

  • LNTH-2403 (Lantheus), a lutetium-177-labelled anti-LRRC15 antibody, in a phase 1/2 trial in relapsed or refractory osteosarcoma (NCT07357519): recruiting since February 2026.
  • ZL-6201 (Zai Lab), an LRRC15 ADC, phase 1a/b in sarcoma and selected solid tumours (NCT07374848): recruiting since March 2026.
  • SOT106 (SOTIO), an LRRC15 ADC, phase 1/2 in LRRC15-positive osteosarcoma and soft-tissue sarcoma (NCT07801222): not yet recruiting (planned start September 2026, not yet updated).
  • LNTH-2403 (Radiopharm Theranostics), an imaging and safety study in colorectal, head and neck, lung and triple-negative breast cancer (NCT07272642): not yet recruiting; it does not list sarcoma. The record names its drug "LNTH-2403 (177Lu-DOTA-DUNP19)", so it is the same lutetium-177 anti-LRRC15 antibody as in Lu-TARGO (registry, November 19, 2026). An earlier version of this report called it "NTH-2403" and could not confirm the match.

Status on November 19, 2026 (registry): Lu-TARGO and ZL-6201 are recruiting; SOT106 is still not yet recruiting; none has posted results. A completed one-patient LRRC15 SPECT imaging study in Wuhan (NCT07651020) adds nothing.

So this is not a new idea, and industry is ahead of the published osteosarcoma literature. The repository's evidence tables held 6 papers naming LRRC15 when this report was built (our calculation); a PubMed search for "LRRC15 AND osteosarcoma" now returns 11, 7 of them from 2025–26 (registry, November 19, 2026). Anyone working on osteosarcoma should know these trials.

Protein evidence (added November 19, 2026). Among the five leads, LRRC15 is the only one measured as protein in patient osteosarcomas: - Cui et al., J Orthop Res 2020 (PMID 32902907; paper, abstract only). On a tissue microarray, 63 of 69 osteosarcomas (91.3%) stained, 51 (74%) at 2+ or 3+ (percentage our calculation). Higher LRRC15 went with more metastasis, chemoresistance and shorter survival. The abstract does not give the antibody or the primary/metastasis split. - O'Reilly et al., Clin Cancer Res 2026 (PMID 41627173; paper, full text). 18 archived paediatric primary tumours stained with clone EPR8188(2): median H-score 113.5, range 0–236. In mice, LRRC15 RNA was limited to hair follicles, marrow mesenchymal stem cells and osteoblasts, and LRRC15 CAR-T cells were active without detectable toxicity; tumours escaped by losing the antigen. - Moquin-Beaudry et al., Nat Commun 2026 (PMID 42399228; paper, full text). Spatial transcriptomics of 26 samples from 19 patients, including metastases and relapses: LRRC15 is part of a 9-gene osteosarcoma surface signature that holds at relapse, and its staining matched its RNA. The authors stress that no single target is expressed throughout a tumour, and give no per-gene positivity rates. - Mooney et al., Cell Rep 2026 (PMID 42647168; paper, full text). Mass-spectrometry surfaceome and global proteome of 22 osteosarcoma patient-derived xenografts: LRRC15 enriched by both methods and among the top hits.

What is still missing: positivity rates in paired primary, lung-metastasis and relapse tissue, and protein in human osteoblasts, growth plate and marrow, which matters most for a lutetium-177 antibody (our inference). Cheapest next step: stain a tissue microarray of paired primary, lung-metastasis and relapse tissue, plus paediatric growth plate and marrow, with the EPR8188(2) clone, scoring H-score and % positive cells. Before that, ask Lantheus and SOTIO whether they will publish their screening staining data; SOT106 enrols only LRRC15-positive tumours, so SOTIO must have an assay.

2. IFITM5: the most interesting new hit, with a built-in caveat

657 TPM in tumours, 88.6% expressing, 2.6 TPM in the worst vital tissue and 0.0 TPM in fibroblasts (our calculation; GTEx registry). IFITM5 (also called BRIL) is an osteoblast membrane protein, and a recurrent variant in it causes osteogenesis imperfecta type V (paper, abstract only). We found no ClinicalTrials.gov study naming it (registry), and the osteosarcoma literature has 9 papers naming it (our calculation). On these numbers it has the cleanest window of any surface protein here that no programme targets yet. The biology is coherent: osteosarcoma is a tumour of the osteoblast lineage. But GTEx has no bone. IFITM5's expression in normal osteoblasts, growth plate and remodelling bone is not in this dataset, and it is unlikely to be zero. A real assessment needs normal bone, especially paediatric growth plate, since the patients are still growing. In short, it is the best-looking new target here, and the one whose risk this screen is least able to see.

There is no human osteosarcoma protein data for IFITM5 (checked November 19, 2026). HPA has no tissue staining for it (registry), PubMed's IFITM5 osteosarcoma papers are case reports, transcriptomics or one cell-line study, and a 2026 spatial study that found IFITM5 in its osteosarcoma surface signature by RNA could not stain it because no validated antibody was available (paper, full text; PMID 42399228). A preprint reports that the osteoblastic cell state is dampened in metastases and after treatment (preprint, abstract only; Tanaka et al., bioRxiv 2026), so an osteoblast protein may be lower in exactly the lesions a drug would treat (our inference). Since no antibody is ready, the cheapest next step is RNA in-situ staining of tumours, metastases and paediatric growth plate.

3. ADAM12: modest expression, good specificity on our main measure

35 TPM in tumours but in 98.9% of them, against 1.8 TPM in the worst vital tissue (a brain region) and 9 TPM in the worst tissue overall (our calculation). HPA gives it Enhanced IHC reliability, the top grade (registry), but that grade rests on three antibodies whose staining patterns disagree: one stains placenta and marrow, one chondrocytes and testis, and one brain, gut and kidney strongly (registry, checked November 19, 2026). We cannot tell which is right, so the grade is uninformative. There is no human osteosarcoma protein data for ADAM12: the only osteosarcoma study is a mouse model (paper, abstract only; PMID 23490646). Fibroblasts sit at 32 TPM, so part of this signal is mesenchymal lineage rather than tumour, and counting fibroblasts drops it to rank 213 (see the sensitivity table). We found no antibody or ADC programme against it; the only registered trial naming ADAM12 uses it as a biomarker (registry). ADAM12 is a sheddase involved in invasion, which also means a soluble form may circulate and soak up an antibody. That is worth checking before investing.

4. PTK7: the one you could test fastest

93 TPM in tumours, 98.9% expressing, 39 TPM in lung as the worst vital tissue (our calculation). The window is modest (2.3-fold), and PTK7 is also 116 TPM in uterus and 72 TPM in fibroblasts, so its rank depends on which tissues count. What makes it a lead is that three PTK7-directed ADCs are in phase 1 now: HWK-007 (NCT07444814), KIVU-107 (NCT07229313) and DAY301 (NCT06752681), all recruiting (registry). They follow Pfizer's earlier PTK7 ADC, PF-06647020, whose trials are completed (NCT02222922; NCT03243331, with gedatolisib); the later AbbVie trial of the same drug, cofetuzumab pelidotin, in PTK7-expressing lung cancer (NCT04189614) was terminated for "strategic considerations" (registry). KIVU-107 lists sarcoma among eligible tumours. We have no evidence that any sarcoma patient has been dosed. Two more PTK7 programmes were found on November 19, 2026 (registry): IDE034 (IDEAYA, NCT07503808), a B7-H3 × PTK7 bispecific ADC in carcinomas whose tumours express both antigens, recruiting; and LY4175408 (Lilly, NCT07046923), active and not recruiting, which is possibly PTK7-directed (inferred): the record does not name its target, but it excludes patients who had a PTK7 ADC with a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload. Neither lists osteosarcoma.

PTK7's protein evidence is partial: two osteosarcoma xenografts (one from a relapse) and two cell lines stained positive, and a paediatric normal-tissue array was negative or weak in every organ (paper, full text; Lee et al., Cell Rep Med 2023, PMID 37343516), and the 2026 xenograft proteomics study names it (paper, full text). No series of patient osteosarcomas has been stained for PTK7. Cheapest next step: stain an osteosarcoma tissue array with the clinical-trial PTK7 assay and cut-off. Fewer than five osteosarcoma papers in our PubTator-tagged set name PTK7 (our calculation), and a PubMed search for "PTK7 AND osteosarcoma" returns one record, from 2008 (registry, November 20, 2026). Testing an existing drug is a cheaper experiment than building one for a new antigen.

5. CD248 / endosialin (TEM1): an antibody already tested, and it did not help in sarcoma

164 TPM, 100% of tumours, 80 TPM in coronary artery (our calculation). The window is the weakest of the five, and the fibroblast value of 578 TPM is a warning: CD248 is a pericyte and activated-fibroblast antigen, so an antibody may hit stroma as much as tumour. Its rank also collapses when all 54 tissues are used (98 to 1,230).

The anti-CD248 antibody ontuxizumab (MORAb-004) has already been tested, and the results are published: - Children: a Children's Oncology Group phase 1 trial (NCT01748721) treated 27 patients aged 3–21 with relapsed or refractory solid tumours. 12 mg/kg weekly was tolerated, with dose-limiting bacteraemia and hyponatraemia in 2 of 10 patients at that dose, and drug levels similar to adults' (paper, abstract only; Norris et al., Pediatr Blood Cancer 2018, PMID 29292843). The abstract reports no responses and does not say how many patients had osteosarcoma. - Adult soft-tissue sarcoma: a randomised phase 2 trial in 209 patients found no benefit from adding ontuxizumab to gemcitabine and docetaxel: progression-free survival 4.3 against 5.6 months, hazard ratio 1.07 (paper, abstract only; Jones et al., Cancer 2019, PMID 31034598). Phase 2 trials in melanoma (PMID 29127533) and colorectal cancer (PMID 29084918) were also published.

So a plain anti-CD248 antibody was safe in children and did not work in a randomised sarcoma trial. CD248 stays on the list only as an expression observation. Any new attempt would need a different kind of agent, such as an ADC, and a reason to expect a different result.

Also worth a look

MRC2 (Endo180/uPARAP; 436 TPM, 100% of tumours, Enhanced IHC) is a collagen-internalising receptor. It internalises by design, which is what an ADC needs. We found no clinical programme, but preclinical MRC2 ADCs exist, including one developed for sarcoma. Evans et al., Mol Cancer Ther 2023 (PMID 36399638) reported tumour regression and less metastatic spread to lungs, liver and lymph nodes in a sarcoma xenograft. They also reported strong MRC2 staining in many of 625 soft-tissue sarcomas, and gene-expression data showing higher MRC2 in soft-tissue and bone sarcomas than in normal tissue (paper, abstract only). Earlier uPARAP ADC work dates from 2017 (PMID 28574834), and a 2026 mesothelioma study followed (PMID 41366685). The fibroblast value of 259 TPM is the concern.

CD109 (38 TPM, 96.6%, Enhanced IHC; fewer than five osteosarcoma papers; no programme found) is a GPI-anchored TGF-β co-receptor. ULBP2 is an NKG2D ligand with a clean window (17 against 6.7 TPM). It is interesting for NK-cell approaches rather than ADCs, but it is stress-induced and shed, so surface levels are unstable. EPHB2 and EPHA3 both score above EPHA2 (1.05 and 0.73 against −0.94). ROBO1 has three CAR-NK trials (NCT03931720, NCT03941457, NCT03940820), all from one sponsor, started in 2019 and now of unknown status (registry), which is a reason for caution.

Candidates that are probably artefacts

CD84, CSF1R and ITGA4 are immune-cell receptors. Osteosarcoma biopsies contain many macrophages and other immune cells, so these signals may come from those cells, not tumour. HPA classes CSF1R as "immune cell enhanced" (registry). CD84 is classed as "low immune cell specificity", so the argument for CD84 rests on its known biology as a leukocyte SLAM-family receptor, not on HPA. CSF1R is a macrophage marker; targeting it is a tumour-microenvironment strategy, not an antigen strategy. SDC2, EMP3, SLC1A5 and SLC16A1 are broadly expressed and their windows are under 2-fold.

The housekeeping filter matters more than its size suggests. Eight housekeeping-flagged genes fall within the first 30 places of the unfiltered list (NDC1 at 4, CYP20A1, TMEM50A, LAPTM4A at 13, MPZL1, CD320, TMEM65, NUS1). Most are not plausible antibody targets: NDC1 is a nuclear-pore protein that passes the surface filter only because HPA lists the plasma membrane as a secondary location.

Caveats

These are ordered by how much they should change your reading.

RNA is not protein. Everything here is transcript abundance. Antigen density on the cell surface, which is what an ADC or CAR actually sees, can differ greatly from mRNA levels and depends on trafficking, shedding and turnover. The HPA columns are the only protein-level evidence in this report. They say "this protein has been detected somewhere", not "this protein is on the surface of osteosarcoma cells at a given density". No candidate here is actionable without immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry on osteosarcoma tissue.

The normal-tissue reference contains no bone. GTEx's 54 tissues include no bone, cartilage, periosteum or growth plate. For a tumour of the osteoblast lineage this is the biggest gap: the normal cells most likely to share a surface antigen with the tumour are not in the comparison. IFITM5 and ALPL are directly affected. The windows of bone-restricted antigens are overstated.

GTEx is adult; the patients are children. Most GTEx donors are middle-aged or older. Osteosarcoma occurs at the adolescent growth spurt, when bone is remodelling fast. A target that is quiet in adult tissue may be active in a growing child.

TARGET-OS is small and nearly all diagnostic biopsies. 88 samples, all primary tumour. Osteosarcoma kills mainly through lung metastases that appear after chemotherapy, and antigen expression can change under treatment and at metastatic sites. Nothing here shows whether these targets are still expressed in relapsed, metastatic disease. With n = 88, the 95% confidence interval on a "% of tumours expressing" figure is about ±6 points near 90% and ±10 points near 50% (our calculation).

Bulk RNA-seq cannot separate tumour from stroma or immune cells. A high bulk TPM can come from tumour cells, fibroblasts, pericytes or macrophages, and the therapeutic consequences are very different. Single-cell data would resolve this and were not used.

The surface annotation is noisy in both directions. HPA's immunofluorescence missed CD276 (it saw only vesicles; CD276 is rescued only as a curated CD marker) and placed MMP14 and CDH11 in the low tier. That is why the headline list uses a loose tier and prints it. The intended cross-checks, the Cell Surface Protein Atlas / SURFY surfaceome and UniProt subcellular locations, could not be reached from this environment, so there is one annotation source where there should be three.

The score is a heuristic, and the comparison is not a validation. The log-ratio form, pseudocount, thresholds and the choice of vital tissues are judgement calls, and the comparison genes were chosen by us. The sensitivity table above shows which conclusions survive other choices. The top-25 list is the extreme tail of 2,434 ratios estimated from 88 samples, so ranks below the top few are not stable.

Expression is necessary but far from sufficient. A real target also needs to internalise (for an ADC), not be shed as a soluble decoy, be expressed evenly within a tumour, and be absent from blood stem cells. None of that is assessed here. ADAM12, CD248 and ULBP2 have known shed or soluble forms.

DepMap was not checked. The plan included confirming that osteosarcoma cell lines express the top candidates. depmap.org returned a bot-verification page instead of data from this environment, and the Sanger Cell Model Passports API was blocked by the proxy. This step was not done.

Literature counts are incomplete by construction. The repository's gene table includes only genes named in at least five osteosarcoma papers, so "fewer than five" is the most we can say for some genes. The counts also inherit the text-mining limits described in the literature map.

The trial search matches text, not curated target fields. ClinicalTrials.gov has no structured "target" field, so a target is counted when its name appears in a study's text. That misses programmes that never name the target and can over-count studies that mention a gene only as a biomarker. The specific NCT numbers quoted above were read individually on November 14, 2026.

Reproducing this

python3 tools/surface_targets.py all # or: gdc hpa gtex rank hpadetail context sensitivity python3 tools/surface_targets.py sensitivity # re-rank the published CSV under other choices

Stages cache into data/surface_targets_raw/ (git-ignored, about 350 MB of GDC files), so re-runs are cheap. GTEx's bulk median-TPM files returned 403/404 here, so normals come through the API gene by gene. The GTEx API defaults to v10, but its gene-lookup endpoint returns only GENCODE v26 identifiers, so everything is pinned to GTEx v8.

What would make this real

In rough order of value per unit effort:

  1. Immunohistochemistry on an osteosarcoma tissue microarray for IFITM5, MRC2, CD109 and ADAM12. This is the step that would turn these hypotheses into evidence. Update, November 19, 2026: one array of primaries with paired lung metastases and relapses, plus paediatric growth plate, stained for LRRC15, PTK7, TMEM119 and CD109, with IFITM5 by RNA in-situ staining (no validated antibody exists) and ADAM12 with two HPA antibodies side by side, would settle most of the open protein questions. COG's and St. Jude's osteosarcoma arrays already exist. For MRC2, start from the published sarcoma staining (PMID 36399638).
  2. Normal paediatric bone and growth-plate expression for IFITM5 and any other bone-restricted candidate. Without it, the top of this list cannot be assessed for safety.
  3. Follow the LRRC15 trials, especially the recruiting osteosarcoma trial of LNTH-2403 (NCT07357519).
  4. Ask whether PTK7 ADCs can be tested in osteosarcoma. Three are in phase 1, plus the B7-H3 × PTK7 conjugate IDE034, and KIVU-107 already lists sarcoma. This needs no new molecule.
  5. Single-cell or spatial data to settle which signals are tumour and which are stroma or macrophage.
  6. Relapsed and metastatic samples, since that is the disease that needs treating.
