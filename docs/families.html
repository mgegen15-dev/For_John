For Families

For parents, patients and anyone supporting someone with osteosarcoma. Written in plain words from this project's reports. Not medical advice: nothing here replaces your care team, and every decision about treatment belongs with them. November 2026.

What This Project Is

I'm Michael. Osteosarcoma took my little brother, John, in 2025, when he was 18. This project uses AI tools to read the published research, the trial registries and public data, look for what has been missed, and test new ideas in the open. It is not a clinic, and it does not recommend treatments. What it can do is help you ask sharper questions.

Questions Worth Bringing to Your Care Team

These come straight from what the reports found. Your team will know which apply.

About the tumour itself

  • Has the tumour had a gene test (often called a gene panel, sequencing or molecular profiling)? If so, may we have a copy of the report?
  • Does it show a deletion of CDKN2A or MTAP, or extra copies (amplification) of CCNE1? Drugs that pick patients by these changes are in trials now. About 1 in 5 relapsed osteosarcomas have the first and about 1 in 10 the second.
  • MTAP often is not on the standard test. Could it be checked with a newer test or a tissue stain?
  • These changes usually match between the first tumour and a relapse, so the test from diagnosis is a fair guide. Is a new biopsy needed, or can the existing sample be used?

If the cancer has come back

  • Are there clinical trials we should consider, here or elsewhere?
  • Some trials start at age 18, but some take patients from 12. Does the age limit rule my child out, and is there a trial that does not?
  • Are kinase-inhibitor pills (such as cabozantinib or regorafenib) an option? In published relapse studies they kept about 40 to 50% of patients free of progression at four months, though the effect tends to fade.

What the Research Found, in Plain Words

  • Most osteosarcoma trials that stop early stop because they cannot find enough patients, not because the drug failed. Joining a trial helps the next family as well as your own.
  • Newer targeted drugs mostly test adults. Of 34 such trials that would take an osteosarcoma patient on paper, only two accept anyone under 18. We are asking sponsors to change that (brief).
  • Several promising ideas have not yet reached patients, such as blocking inflammation after surgery to keep the cancer out of the lungs. They need laboratory and animal work first (ranked leads).
  • Some published research is unreliable. If you read papers yourself, be wary of single small studies, especially about microRNAs, where retraction rates are high.

Finding Trials

  • ClinicalTrials.gov lists every registered trial. Search "osteosarcoma" and filter by "Recruiting" and by age. Each listing has a contact.
  • Your care team can tell you which trials are realistic, and can contact trial sites for you.
  • Osteosarcoma organisations such as MIB Agents and the Osteosarcoma Institute support families and follow the research.
  • The trial lists in this project: trials for tumours missing MTAP and trials for tumours with extra CCNE1, with registry numbers to give your team. They were correct on November 24, 2026, and trials change.

Words You Will Hear

  • Relapse or recurrence: the cancer has come back after treatment.
  • Metastasis: the cancer has spread, most often to the lungs.
  • Gene panel or sequencing: a test of the tumour's DNA that lists the genes it has changed.
  • Deletion: a gene is missing from the tumour. Amplification: the tumour has extra copies of a gene.
  • Phase 1 trial: the first test of a drug in people, mainly to find a safe dose. Phase 2: testing whether it works. Phase 3: comparing it with the current standard.
  • Progression-free: the cancer has not grown.
